benchbit / abstract.txt

BenchBit · B.Sc. thesis · TUM

Abstract

Designing blockchain systems for decentralized applications requires that software architects address non-functional needs, which include balancing decentralization, scalability, and security, commonly referred to as the blockchain trilemma. Achieving a good balance among the three axes is challenging due to the lack of empirical data on their trade-offs.

Existing studies are mostly conceptual or focus primarily on the Proof-of-Stake consensus mechanism, leaving a gap in empirical quantification for Proof-of-Work systems and hindering the identification of optimal system configurations.

To address this, I developed BenchBit, a Bitcoin benchmarking framework deployed on the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre (LRZ) cluster. This thesis involved 216 experimental runs across 36 different configurations, varying the block interval, block maximum weight, and node count, among others, on a private Bitcoin network. The data was analyzed using correlation, ANOVA, and Pareto analysis to understand the impact of the configuration parameters.

Key results include a weak but statistically significant negative correlation between decentralization, measured by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index or the Gini coefficient, and scalability, measured by throughput. While security metrics, in particular the stale block rate, were less effective due to data sparsity in short observation windows during experiments and the orchestrated mining model, Pareto analysis revealed that within the selected testing ranges, configurations with a 30-second block interval, 12 nodes, and uniform hash distribution consistently appeared on all Pareto fronts and offered the most advantageous balance across the trilemma, simultaneously maximizing throughput and security (cost of attack) while maintaining high decentralization. Regarding security, the thesis demonstrates that probabilistic metrics such as the stale block rate (SBR) become zero-inflated in short observation windows, making them unsuitable for short-term benchmarking. Consequently, the framework establishes that security quantification in such environments may require either another security metric, such as modeled proxies (cost of attack), or significantly extended observation windows.

This thesis presents BenchBit, an automated benchmarking platform for PoW systems on high performance computing (HPC) infrastructure, along with a reusable analytical pipeline for correlation, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and Pareto analysis, aiding architects in moving from raw data to actionable design decisions.
